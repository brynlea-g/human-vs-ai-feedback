AI versus Human Feedback: How Perceived and Actual Source Shapes Student Preferences and Learning Outcomes.

Brynlea Gibson & Jason Tangen · School of Psychology, The University of Queensland

Students struggled to tell AI feedback from human feedback. They rated the feedback they judged to be human more highly, yet the feedback actually written by AI scored higher.

Why this study

Does it matter who wrote the feedback, or who students think wrote it?

AI systems now write feedback on student work, and students increasingly know it. Past research pulls in two directions: people sometimes distrust algorithms (algorithm aversion) and sometimes prefer them (algorithm appreciation). Studies that reveal the source also find that an "AI" label can lower how students rate the same feedback.

We separated two things that usually travel together: the actual source of the feedback, and the source students believed it came from.

What participants did

One hour, two topics, two sources

160 UQ psychology undergraduates completed the study in the lab. Participants did not write the first answer themselves: they read a weak answer prepared for the study, then improved it.

  1. Read a weak answerA short answer to an introductory psychology question, written for the study with a preset mix of grammar, flow and conceptual errors, including a mix-up with a commonly confused concept.
  2. Receive feedbackWritten by a paid UQ course tutor or generated by an AI system (GPT-5.5 or Claude Sonnet 4.6), using the same rubric. The source was never shown.
  3. Rewrite the answerUsing the feedback, with no time or word limit.
  4. Rate the task and the feedback0–100 sliders: confidence and difficulty first, then helpfulness, clarity, tone and style, and memorability and impact in a random order.
  5. Repeat with the other sourceA second topic with feedback from the other source. Source order was counterbalanced and topics were randomised.
  6. Judge the sourceAt the end, students learned that each piece of feedback had a 50% chance of coming from a human or AI, then judged each one: AI, human, or not sure.

2Believed source

Feedback believed to be human-written was rated higher than perceived AI

Rating difference (human − AI) on a 0–100 scale, with 95% confidence intervals. 259 ratings from the 146 students who gave a definite judgement.

● significant   ○ not significant (Holm)Tap a point for details
Feedback students thought was human-written scored 6 to 7 points higher for clarity, tone and memorability. The helpfulness gap was not significant.
Reading this result. Students reported their source beliefs after both rounds, so this is an association, not a causal effect of belief. It could also run the other way: students may have labelled the feedback they liked as "human".
3Actual source

But when comparing actual source, AI-written feedback was rated higher

Rating difference (human − AI) on a 0–100 scale, with 95% confidence intervals. 320 ratings from all 160 students.

● significant (Holm)Tap a point for details
In our study, AI-written feedback scored 6.5 to 12.5 points higher than tutor feedback on every dimension, with the largest gap for helpfulness.
The paradox

Students rated the feedback they judged to be human more highly, yet the feedback actually written by AI was rated higher.

For the stats-curious

How we analysed it, and how robust it is

We fitted four linear mixed-effects models, one per rating, with random intercepts for participant and topic. Positive estimates mean human feedback was rated higher. We applied a Holm correction across the four ratings as an analysis choice.

Both effects in one model (exploratory)

Because belief and reality often disagreed, we entered both into the same model. Both effects held on all four ratings, including helpfulness, and both grew slightly. They do not simply cancel each other out.

RatingBelieved humanActually human
Helpfulness+6.3−14.3
Clarity+7.6−8.2
Tone and style+8.0−8.8
Memorability and impact+9.1−9.4

All Holm-adjusted p < .01. Belief was measured after exposure, so these are adjusted associations, not causal or mediation effects.

Robustness checks
  • Topic as a fixed effect (only four topics): estimates barely moved, e.g. believed clarity +6.1, actual helpfulness −12.6.
  • Adding round (first vs second) to check for practice or fatigue: the same pattern held, with believed helpfulness still not significant.
  • Treating "unsure" as its own category: feedback judged "unsure" was not rated differently from feedback judged AI (all p > .08).

In progress

Planned analysis: feedback effectiveness and marking performance

Pending ethics approval, GPT and Claude will independently assess participants' initial and revised written explanations using a standardised 20-point rubric, alongside expert human marking of a subset of responses.

Performance will be measured as improvement in scores following feedback, allowing us to examine whether feedback attributed to AI versus humans leads to different levels of improvement in conceptual understanding.

Linear mixed-effects models will account for repeated responses within participants and variation across psychology topics. Agreement with expert human marks and consistency across repeated LLM assessments will also be examined to evaluate marking reliability.

This will extend our findings beyond perceived feedback quality (attitudes) to investigate actual improvements in understanding (performance).
Good to know

Limits of these results

  • These are preliminary results, not yet peer reviewed.
  • The AI advantage reflects the feedback texts in this study (two tutors and two AI models per topic), not AI feedback in general.
  • Source beliefs were collected after both rounds, so the believed-source results are associations.
